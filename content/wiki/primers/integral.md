The integral of a function over an interval is the signed area between its
graph and the horizontal axis — area above the axis counted positive, area
below counted negative. It is defined by approximation: slice the interval
into strips, sum the rectangles, and take the limit as the slicing becomes
arbitrarily fine. The notation $\int_a^b f(x)\,dx$ names that limiting
value.

For present purposes the integral is best thought of as a machine for
averaging and accumulating: it condenses a function's behaviour over a whole
interval into a single number. It is linear — the integral of a sum is the
sum of the integrals — and it is blind to behaviour at isolated points, which
is why arguments that integrate can tolerate functions with jumps.
